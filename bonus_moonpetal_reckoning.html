The Moonpetal Reckoning

A recipe you can't read backward: three shimmer per moonpetal, on a five-shimmer springwater base, and the potion is finished when the shimmer reads exactly 23. How many petals? Today you find out two ways: by reckoning it on parchment, and by brewing it in the cauldron.

3 × PETALS + 5 = 23

YOU'LL NEED: Days 1–2 complete · a new file saved as reckoning.py · your Grimoire · Day 3's def for Section III

Today's target

I can make a loop search for the value that makes a statement true, and say what stops it. E5-PRO-VD-?? ⚠

I can compare two searches for the same answer and defend which one I trust. E5-PRO-RD-?? ⚠

The apothecary's law Moonpetals are never cut. Half a petal is not a measurement, so every answer today is a whole number of petals, or there is no answer at all.
Chunks explored
Section I

The Reckoning: work it out on parchment

No cauldron yet. You test petal counts one at a time and ask, each time, whether the recipe would come out right.

Chunk 1 · The recipe is a ruleE5-PRO-VD-?? ⚠

Three per petal, on a base of five

Day 2 turned a rule into orb positions. The same kind of rule reads a recipe: 3 * petals + 5 tells you the shimmer for any petal count.

petals = 4
print(3 * petals + 5)
print(3 * petals + 5 == 23)
Predict Two lines print. What shimmer does 4 petals make, and what does the second line answer? Then work out on your Grimoire which petal count would hit 23 exactly.
Four petals make 17, and the second line answers False, because 17 is not 23. That == is the whole idea of today: it turns a recipe into a question the wand can answer. Six petals is the count that hits 23, and in a moment you'll make a loop find that for you instead of finding it yourself.

Chunk 2 · A vessel that holds an answerE5-PRO-VD-?? ⚠

found: True and False can be bottled

Every vessel so far held a number or some text. A vessel can also hold True or False directly, with no quotation marks. This one remembers whether the search has succeeded.

found = False
print(found)
found = True
print(found)
Predict What two lines print? And is found here holding the word "False", or something else?
False, then True. And it is not the word: "False" with quotes would be text, five letters the wand can print but not reason about. Bare False is an answer, the same kind == hands back. A vessel holding one of these is called a flag: it starts down, and something raises it.

Chunk 3 · Two questions, one gateE5-PRO-RD-?? ⚠

and: both must be yes

Today's one new word. The Atelier taught or, where any yes opens the door. and is stricter: every question must answer yes, or the door stays shut.

found = False
petals = 3
print(found == False and petals <= 20)
print(found == False and petals <= 2)
Predict Two answers. In the second line, one side is yes and the other is no. What does and do with that?
True, then False. One no is enough to shut an and gate. You're about to put this exact line at the top of a while, which gives that loop two ways to stop: the flag goes up, or the petals run out. Both are visible in the question, which is how this Academy likes its exits.

Chunk 4 · The ReckoningE5-PRO-VD-?? ⚠

Let the loop try every count

Here is the search. The cauldron in the corner holds twenty petals and no more, so testing past 20 would be testing a potion nobody can brew. That's where the 20 comes from; it is a fact about the room, not a number picked from the air.

petals = 0
found = False
while found == False and petals <= 20:
    if 3 * petals + 5 == 23:
        found = True
    else:
        petals = petals + 1

print(found)
print(petals)
Predict Trace the first four passes on your Grimoire: what is petals, what does the recipe compute, and what does the gate answer? Which pass raises the flag, and what prints at the end?
The passes compute 5, 8, 11, 14, 17, 20, then 23 on the seventh, where petals is 6 and the flag goes up. It prints True and 6. Look closely at the shape: when the gate says no, the else adds a petal, and that is the line moving the loop toward its exit. When the gate finally says yes, nothing is added, the flag rises, and the question at the top turns no. Six moonpetals.

Chunk 5 · Misfire on purposeE5-PRO-TR-?? ⚠

When the limit lies

Two experiments, one change each. First change the target from 23 to 24. Then change it to 68. Print found and petals both times.

    if 3 * petals + 5 == 24:      # then try 68
Predict For 24: is there a whole number of petals that works? For 68: work out 3 * 21 + 5 on your Grimoire before running. What will the Reckoning report, and will it be telling the truth?
For 24, the Reckoning reports False and it is right: 24 needs six petals and a third, and petals are never cut. For 68 it also reports False, and this time it is lying. Twenty-one petals make exactly 68; the answer was one step past the edge of the search. A limit keeps a loop safe and can also hide the truth, so the honest report is not "there is no answer" but "no answer up to 20 petals." When you set a limit, you are promising something about where you looked.

Section II

The Brewing: watch the shimmer climb

Put the parchment down. Drop petals in one at a time and watch the cauldron's shimmer rise toward the mark.

Chunk 6 · The BrewingE5-PRO-VD-?? ⚠

No flag, no limit, no lie

The cauldron starts at five shimmer from the springwater. Each petal adds three. The loop watches the shimmer instead of testing counts.

shimmer = 5
petals = 0
while shimmer < 23:
    shimmer = shimmer + 3
    petals = petals + 1

print(shimmer)
print(petals)
Predict Trace the shimmer: write every reading from 5 until the loop stops. How many petals went in? And here is the important one: could this loop ever run forever?
The shimmer reads 5, 8, 11, 14, 17, 20, 23, and six petals went in. Same answer as the Reckoning, and notice what this version does not need: no flag, no and, no limit of 20. It cannot run forever, because the shimmer only ever climbs and the target sits above it; every pass moves the loop closer to its exit. Fewer parts, and nothing arbitrary to defend.

Chunk 7 · The overshootE5-PRO-RD-?? ⚠

The needle steps over the mark

Change the target to 24, the potion with no whole-petal answer, and add Day 1's flat gate after the loop to read the result out loud.

shimmer = 5
petals = 0
while shimmer < 24:
    shimmer = shimmer + 3
    petals = petals + 1

if shimmer == 24:
    print(f"Brewed with {petals} moonpetals.")
else:
    print(f"No whole petal count reaches 24. The shimmer jumped to {shimmer}.")
Predict What is the last shimmer reading before the loop stops, and what is the first one after? Which message prints?
The shimmer reads 23, still under 24, so the loop runs once more and lands on 26, stepping clean over the mark. The gate reports no whole-petal answer, and it can prove it by naming the reading it overshot to. You have seen this before: Day 2's charge started at 5, poured 10 at a time, and ended at 35 instead of 30. Same overshoot, new costume. A while stops after the moment it passes the line, never exactly on it unless the arithmetic happens to land there.

Chunk 8 · Two searches, one answerE5-PRO-RD-?? ⚠

Which one would you trust?

Read both openings side by side. Run nothing.

# The Reckoning tests counts:
while found == False and petals <= 20:

# The Brewing watches a reading:
while shimmer < 23:
Predict How many ways can each loop stop? Which one needs a number that somebody chose? Which one told a lie in Chunk 5, and could the other one have made that mistake?
The Reckoning has two exits, the raised flag and the exhausted limit, and that limit is somebody's choice; it lied about 68 petals because the answer lived past the edge of the search. The Brewing has one exit and no chosen number, so it cannot make that mistake, and it finds 21 petals for 68 without being told where to look. The Reckoning earns its keep when you must test a rule that doesn't simply climb. When the value you're chasing rises steadily toward the target, watch the value; it's less code, and there's nothing arbitrary to defend.
Section III

The Reckoning, Bottled

One section from Day 3's spellbook: if def hasn't reached you yet, brew on and come back after. The search from Section I becomes a spell you can cast on any recipe.

Chunk 9 · Bottle the ReckoningE5-PRO-RD-?? ⚠

def reckon(target, cap): the search becomes a spell

You have now retyped this search three times with three different targets. That is the signal to bottle it. Two parameters do it: the target you're chasing, and, straight out of Chunk 5's lesson, the cap that says where the search is allowed to look.

def reckon(target, cap):
    petals = 0
    found = False
    while found == False and petals <= cap:
        if 3 * petals + 5 == target:
            found = True
        else:
            petals = petals + 1
    if found == True:
        print(f"{target} shimmer: brewed with {petals} moonpetals.")
    else:
        print(f"{target} shimmer: no whole-petal answer up to {cap} petals.")

reckon(23, 20)
reckon(24, 20)
reckon(26, 20)
reckon(68, 20)
reckon(68, 25)
Predict Write all five report lines before you run, using your Section I traces. The last two calls ask the same target with different caps: what changes, and which Chunk 5 problem does that cure?
The first four report 6 petals, no answer up to 20, 7 petals, and no answer up to 20; the fifth finds 21 moonpetals, because a wider cap put the answer inside the search. That is Chunk 5's lie, cured by a parameter: the caller now states where to look, and the failure message names that promise instead of pretending the whole world was searched. Notice too that petals and found live inside the spell: fresh vessels at every casting, gone when it ends, the spell's own workbench, just like the parameter vessels from Day 3. Five experiments, five short lines. That is what a def buys.

Build brief · The Apothecary's Ledger

One recipe, any target, both searches

Build a Ledger that asks an apprentice for a target shimmer with input() and int(), then:

1. Runs the Brewing on that target and reports either the petal count or the overshoot, using Chunk 7's gate.
2. Prints the shimmer reading at every step so the ledger shows its work.
3. Names its own honesty: if the potion can't be brewed, say why in the message.

Stretch: add your own recipe, a different shimmer-per-petal and a different base, and find a target that works for yours but not for the moonpetal recipe.

Suggested start: Chunk 6's loop, then the input, then the gate. House law: one change per run, and point at the line that moves the loop toward its exit before you run it.

print("The Apothecary's Ledger")
print("Recipe: 3 shimmer per moonpetal, on a base of 5.")

target = int(input("Target shimmer: "))

shimmer = 5
petals = 0
print(f"  0 petals: shimmer {shimmer}")
while shimmer < target:
    shimmer = shimmer + 3
    petals = petals + 1
    print(f"  {petals} petals: shimmer {shimmer}")

if shimmer == target:
    print(f"Brewed. {petals} moonpetals bring the shimmer to exactly {target}.")
elif petals == 0:
    print(f"Cannot be brewed. The springwater already glows at {shimmer}, past a target of {target}.")
else:
    print(f"Cannot be brewed. The shimmer stepped from {shimmer - 3} to {shimmer}.")
Try 23, then 24, then 68, and then 3. The middle door answers targets the springwater overshoots on its own: zero petals poured means no step to report, so the Ledger refuses without inventing one. It never needs a limit either, because the shimmer always climbs toward the target. Write in your Grimoire why that makes it safe.

On the mathematics: no CCSS-M code is claimed on this page. Solving for an unknown that makes an equation true belongs to the Grade 6 expressions-and-equations progression, not Grade 5; what happens here is guess-and-check against a rule, which supports later algebra without standing in for it. Confirm at source before claiming otherwise.

Deliberately not covered here: today adds one new word, and, and one new kind of value, the bare True/False flag. while not found is the phrasing an experienced programmer would write; this page uses found == False because it reads aloud as a question apprentices already know how to ask, and not is left for later. The bottled reckon spell reports by printing, because return values remain excluded; at this Academy a def answers out loud. break stays excluded, which is exactly why the Reckoning needs its flag; the comparison in Chunk 8 is more valuable than the shortcut would have been. Decimals are excluded on purpose: 0.1 + 0.2 does not equal 0.3 in Python, so == stops being trustworthy the moment fractions enter, and the apothecary's law keeps the arithmetic whole.
