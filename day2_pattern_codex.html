Chunk 1 · Pattern: the accumulatorE5-PRO-VD-?? ⚠

The gathering charm

The most useful pattern in programming: start a vessel at 0, then let a loop pour into it, one pass at a time.

sparks = [12, 7, 20]
total = 0
for s in sparks:
    total = total + s
print("Sparks gathered:", total)
Predict Trace it on your Grimoire sheet: what does total hold after each pass? What prints at the end?
total goes 0, then 12, then 19, then 39. The line total = total + s reads strangely at first: pour into the vessel its own contents plus one more spark. Every running total you have ever seen, from game scores to shopping carts, is this exact charm.

Chunk 2 · The mirror and its shadowE5-PRO-RD-?? ⚠

== asks the same; != asks different

One equals sign pours a value in. Two equals signs ask "are these the same?" The shadow twin != asks "are these different?" The wand answers True or False.

word = "ember"
print(word == "ember")
print(word == "Ember")
print(word != "frost")
Predict Three answers will print. Write all three, and say why the second differs from the first.
True, False, True. The wand matches letters exactly, and a capital E is a different letter. Read != out loud as "is not" and it stops being slippery. Mixing up = and == is one of the most common misfires in programming; now you can name both halves.

Chunk 3 · Pattern: the counter with a gateE5-PRO-VD-?? ⚠

Count only the strong ones

An accumulator that adds 1 instead of the value, and only when a gate says yes.

sparks = [12, 7, 20, 9]
strong = 0
for s in sparks:
    if s > 10:
        strong = strong + 1
print("Strong sparks:", strong)
Predict Which sparks pass the gate? What does strong hold at the end?
12 and 20 pass; 7 and 9 don't. strong ends at 2. Notice the shape: a loop, a gate inside it, a counter inside the gate. Three Day-1 tools, one new pattern.

Chunk 4 · Pattern: best-so-farE5-PRO-VD-?? ⚠

The brightest spark

Keep a champion vessel. Every pass, a challenger arrives; the gate decides whether the champion is replaced.

sparks = [12, 7, 20, 9]
brightest = 0
for s in sparks:
    if s > brightest:
        brightest = s
print("Brightest spark:", brightest)
Predict Trace brightest through all four passes. On which passes does it change, and what does it hold at the end?
It changes on pass 1 (0 becomes 12) and pass 3 (12 becomes 20); passes 2 and 4 leave it alone because 7 and 9 lose the challenge. Final answer: 20. Swap the > for < and the same pattern finds the dimmest spark instead.

Chunk 5 · Meet whileE5-PRO-VD-?? ⚠

The growing charm: watch, don't count

for counts. while watches: it checks its question at the top of every pass, and runs the indented lines only while the answer is yes.

power = 10
while power < 50:
    power = power + 10
    print(power)
print("The charm is charged.")
Predict Trace it: what numbers print, and does 50 itself appear? When does the last line finally run?
20, 30, 40, and yes, 50 prints. The check happens at the top: 40 is still less than 50, so the loop runs once more, pours in 10, and prints 50. Only then does the check fail. Notice the pour is Chunk 1's accumulator; a while is often an accumulator with a finish line.

Chunk 6 · Misfire on purposeE5-PRO-TR-?? ⚠

The unending curse, and how to stop it

A while must move toward no. Break that law on purpose, once, so you know the counter-curse. Before you run this, find the stop control: the square stop button in the terminal's corner, or click in the terminal and press Ctrl+C.

power = 10
while power > 0:
    power = power + 10
    print(power)
Predict Read the question and the pour. Will the answer ever become no? What is going to happen when you run this?
Never. power starts above 0 and only grows, so the question stays yes forever and numbers pour down the terminal until you end it. Stop it now. This is the unending curse, and every programmer casts it by accident sometimes. The counter-curse is a habit: before running any while, point at the line that moves it toward no. If you can't find one, don't run it.
You should see 
20, 30, 40, 50... climbing forever until you press stop.
Then the terminal reports the spell was interrupted.
Chunk 7 · Pattern: the sentinel gateE5-PRO-TR-?? ⚠

Ask until the right word is spoken

The classic while pattern: keep asking while the answer is wrong. The exit is a word, not a count.

secret = "emberlight"
guess = input("Speak the word: ")
while guess != secret:
    print("The door does not stir.")
    guess = input("Speak the word: ")
print("The door swings open.")
Predict Guess wrong twice, then right. How many times does "The door does not stir." print? Which line moves the loop toward no?
Twice, once per wrong guess. The line that moves it toward no is the second input(), inside the loop: it refills guess so the question can change its answer. Delete that line and the door asks once and sulks forever. The same pattern with while ask not in shelf: guards a whole shelf; you'll cast that one in the build.

Speak the word: frostveil
The door does not stir.
Speak the word: gloomvine
The door does not stir.
Speak the word: emberlight
The door swings open.
Chunk 8 · First LightE5-PRO-PD-?? ⚠

Open a window in nine lines

You installed pygame at the door. Now the moment: a real window, a violet sky, one golden orb. Save this as its own casting and run it.

import pygame

pygame.init()
screen = pygame.display.set_mode((640, 480))
pygame.display.set_caption("First Light")
screen.fill((45, 27, 78))
pygame.draw.circle(screen, (240, 180, 41), (320, 240), 60)
pygame.display.flip()
pygame.time.wait(3000)
pygame.quit()
Predict How long will the window stay open, and what makes it close? The orb sits at (320, 240) in a 640-by-480 window: where on the screen is that?
Three seconds: wait(3000) counts in milliseconds, then the program reaches pygame.quit() and the window closes itself. And (320, 240) is dead center, half of 640 and half of 480. The three color numbers are red, green, and blue, 0 to 255; the sky is the Academy's own violet. Nine lines, and you are officially a graphics programmer.

Chunk 9 · A rule places the orbs5.OA.B.3

The constellation rule

Yesterday a rule wove runes in the terminal. The same idea places orbs in a window: the rule 70 + i * 100 turns the loop number into a position.

import pygame

pygame.init()
screen = pygame.display.set_mode((640, 480))
pygame.display.set_caption("The Constellation Rule")
screen.fill((45, 27, 78))
for i in range(6):
    pygame.draw.circle(screen, (240, 180, 41), (70 + i * 100, 240), 30)
pygame.display.flip()
pygame.time.wait(3000)
pygame.quit()
Predict Fill the ordered-pairs table on your Grimoire sheet: (i, x position) for every pass. Where does the first orb sit, and how far apart are neighbors?
The pairs are (0, 70), (1, 170), (2, 270), (3, 370), (4, 470), (5, 570): start at 70, step by 100. Two patterns move together, the loop number and the position, and the rule is the bridge between them. Change the 100 and the whole constellation breathes in or out.
You should see 
Six golden orbs in a level row across the middle
of the window, evenly spaced.
Chunk 10 · A list feeds the windowE5-PRO-PD-?? ⚠

Orbs sized by the shelf

A list holds the data, a loop reads it, and the window shows it. Spark i gets size sizes[i].

import pygame

pygame.init()
screen = pygame.display.set_mode((640, 480))
pygame.display.set_caption("The Ledger, Drawn")
sizes = [10, 25, 40, 25, 10]
screen.fill((45, 27, 78))
for i in range(5):
    pygame.draw.circle(screen, (240, 180, 41), (70 + i * 125, 240), sizes[i])
pygame.display.flip()
pygame.time.wait(3000)
pygame.quit()
Predict Which orb is biggest, and where does it sit? What one change makes the row shrink toward the middle instead?
The middle orb, at x = 320, because sizes[2] is 40. To flip the shape, reorder the list: [40, 25, 10, 25, 40]. One list drives the whole picture, which is exactly how the engine you'll meet on Day 3 works: its sparks live in lists, and the loop reads them out.

Chunk 11 · The endless castingE5-PRO-RD-?? ⚠

The real game loop: while running

Chunk 5's watcher, grown up. This is the pattern under every game ever made, in five beats: watch, listen, update, draw, breathe. The wisp drifts until you close the window; closing it is the exit.

import pygame

pygame.init()
screen = pygame.display.set_mode((640, 480))
pygame.display.set_caption("The Wisp")
clock = pygame.time.Clock()

x = 0
running = True
while running:                          # 1. watch
    for event in pygame.event.get():    # 2. listen
        if event.type == pygame.QUIT:
            running = False
    x = x + 3                           # 3. update
    if x > 640:
        x = 0
    screen.fill((45, 27, 78))           # 4. draw
    pygame.draw.circle(screen, (240, 180, 41), (x, 240), 20)
    pygame.display.flip()
    clock.tick(60)                      # 5. breathe

pygame.quit()
Predict Which line moves this while toward no, and what do you do to trigger it? What happens to the wisp when x passes 640?
The exit is running = False, and you trigger it by clicking the window's close button; that arrives as a QUIT event, the listener catches it, and the watch question turns no. Until then the wisp drifts right 3 pixels per frame and wraps to 0 at the edge. Notice x = x + 3: the accumulator again, now moving a light across a sky. Every game you've played is this loop with more inside it.
You should see 
A golden wisp gliding left to right across the violet
sky, forever, wrapping at the edge. It stops only when
you close the window yourself.
Hinge question · stop here until you're sureE5-PRO-VD-?? ⚠

The overshooting charm

Part 1: sparks = [5, 10, 2] pours through the accumulator. What does total hold after each pass?
Part 2: charge = 5, then while charge < 30: charge = charge + 10 with a print(charge) inside. What prints, and where does charge end?

Part 1: 5, then 15, then 17. If you said 2, you traced a pour; the accumulator adds. Part 2: 15, 25, 35 print, and charge ends at 35. The check sees 25 < 30, says yes one last time, and the pour overshoots the finish line. If both parts landed, the Codex is yours, counting charms and watching charms alike.
Build brief · The Night Ledger

Count it, guard it, set it drifting

Part A, the Ledger (terminal): your own list of 5–6 spark values, reported in one f-string: the total (accumulator), how many are over 15 (counter with a gate), and the brightest (best-so-far).

Part B, the Vigil door (terminal): your own secret word, a sentinel while gate, an attempts counter (Chunk 3's pattern inside Chunk 7's loop), and an f-string report when it opens.

Part C, the Wandering Wisp (window): your own wisp in the endless casting: your color, your speed. It must name its exit: closing the window ends it cleanly.

Suggested start: A, then B, then C, one pattern per run. House law: one change per run, and point at every while's exit line before you run it.

import pygame

# --- Part A: the ledger ---
sparks = [18, 9, 33, 21, 12]
total = 0
strong = 0
brightest = 0
for s in sparks:
    total = total + s
    if s > 15:
        strong = strong + 1
    if s > brightest:
        brightest = s
print(f"Total: {total}  Over 15: {strong}  Brightest: {brightest}")

# --- Part B: the vigil door ---
secret = "gloamlight"
attempts = 1
guess = input("Speak the word: ")
while guess != secret:
    attempts = attempts + 1
    guess = input("Speak the word: ")
print(f"The door opens after {attempts} attempts. The watch begins.")

# --- Part C: the wandering wisp ---
pygame.init()
screen = pygame.display.set_mode((640, 480))
pygame.display.set_caption("The Night Watch")
clock = pygame.time.Clock()
x = 0
running = True
while running:
    for event in pygame.event.get():
        if event.type == pygame.QUIT:
            running = False
    x = x + 4
    if x > 640:
        x = 0
    screen.fill((16, 8, 34))
    pygame.draw.circle(screen, (196, 120, 255), (x, 240), 18)
    pygame.display.flip()
    clock.tick(60)
pygame.quit()
Three patterns share Part A's loop; yours may use three separate loops, which is just as correct and easier to trace. Why does attempts start at 1, not 0? Answer in your Grimoire.
